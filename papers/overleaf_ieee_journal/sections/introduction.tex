\section{Introduction}

Police data sharing is often discussed as an infrastructure problem. This
paper treats it as an access-governance problem. India already has major
digital policing and criminal-justice infrastructure. The Crime and Criminal
Tracking Network and Systems (CCTNS) is reported by official sources to be in
use across all 17,798 police stations as of 1 February 2026~\cite{pib_cctns_2026}.
The Inter-Operable Criminal Justice System (ICJS) connects the police/CCTNS
pillar with courts, prisons, forensics, and prosecution~\cite{mha_icjs_2026}.
Because of this, the research question is not whether India needs a new
replacement for CCTNS or ICJS. The question is more specific: if sensitive
records move through an inter-agency workflow, how can access decisions be
controlled, audited, and explained in a way that can be reviewed later?

This question matters because police records are not ordinary database rows. A
single request may involve FIR details, victim or witness information, juvenile
records, forensic material, cybercrime complaints, case diary content, evidence
references, or court/prosecution-linked documents. A broad role such as
``police officer'' is not enough to decide access. The same officer may be
allowed to view one record, denied another, and escalated for a third depending
on jurisdiction, case assignment, purpose, sensitivity level, credential
status, approval state, emergency context, and the agency relationship behind
the request.

The problem addressed in this paper is therefore the following. Given a
sensitive inter-agency request for a police or criminal-justice record, the
system should decide whether the request must be allowed, denied, or escalated.
At the same time, it should preserve evidence about the decision: the request
context, the policy version, the attributes that mattered, the explanation
given, and the approval or review event connected to it. The design must avoid
placing raw sensitive records on a shared ledger. It must also avoid assuming
that one mechanism, whether blockchain, access control, or AI, is enough by
itself.

\subsection{Challenges and Contributions}

The paper is written around four practical challenges. Each challenge leads to
one contribution.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Contextual access governance.} Inter-agency police access
    cannot be handled only with static roles. Access depends on the requester,
    record, action, purpose, approval state, and operating context.

    \textbf{Contribution 1:} The paper designs a layered access-control service
    for CCTNS/ICJS-style access requests. The service combines RBAC,
    ABAC, and PBAC-style rules and returns one of three decisions:
    \emph{allow}, \emph{deny}, or \emph{escalate}.

    \item \textbf{Tamper-evident audit across agencies.} A shared audit trail is
    useful only if later reviewers can trust that decision evidence has not
    been silently changed. At the same time, raw sensitive records should not
    be copied to a common blockchain.

    \textbf{Contribution 2:} The paper implements a permissioned
    blockchain-style audit layer that records request identifiers, policy
    versions, decision hashes, explanation hashes, approval references, record
    commitments, and block commitments while keeping raw records off-chain.

    \item \textbf{Explainable access decisions.} When a police officer,
    investigating agency, court-related authority, or auditor requests access
    to a sensitive record, the system should explain why the request was
    allowed, denied, or escalated. This is important because such decisions can
    affect investigation timelines, privacy protection, inter-agency
    accountability, and later legal or administrative review.

    \textbf{Contribution 3:} The paper builds an explainable decision service
    that records the decision label, reason code, decisive attributes,
    policy/rule version, counterfactual information where applicable, and an
    explanation hash linked to the audit event. The explanation is treated as
    an audit artifact, not only as a message shown on a screen.

    \item \textbf{Valid-looking corrupted logs.} A stronger attacker may change
    policy output and then re-sign the log. In that case, the log can look
    internally valid even though the decision has been corrupted.

    \textbf{Contribution 4:} The paper defines and runs a reproducible
    adversarial benchmark for ordinary tamper attacks and a validly re-signed
    compromised-signer attack. It compares mutable logs, signed hash chains,
    blockchain-style audit, CT-style logs, ABAC/Fabric-style re-execution, a
    trusted raw-attribute policy oracle, and NS-PI log-only drift detection.
\end{itemize}

The first contribution is mainly about security and access control. The second
is about blockchain as an audit mechanism. The third is about explainable AI.
The fourth makes the evaluation sharper by testing a failure mode where a log
can remain cryptographically valid but semantically wrong. Together, these
contributions keep the work focused on access governance rather than on broad
claims about police automation.

The access-control layer follows the ABAC idea that decisions can be based on
subject, object, action, and environmental attributes~\cite{nist_abac_2014}.
This fits the police data-sharing setting better than role-only access because
the same role may require different decisions under different purposes,
jurisdictions, and sensitivity levels. The blockchain layer is used narrowly:
it gives tamper-evident audit commitments and provenance. It is not used as a
privacy guarantee, and it does not store raw police records. This is consistent
with the cautious treatment of blockchain access-control systems in
NISTIR~8403~\cite{nist_blockchain_access_2022} and with the permissioned
blockchain model represented by Hyperledger Fabric~\cite{androulaki_fabric_2018}.

The explainable AI layer is also used in a narrow way. It does not explain
crime prediction or suspect prediction. It explains access decisions. For each
request, the system records why the request was allowed, denied, or escalated,
which attributes were decisive, and what policy version was applied. This
follows the broader argument that high-stakes systems should prefer
interpretable and reviewable decisions when possible~\cite{rudin_2019}. It
also follows recent law-enforcement XAI work, which warns that explanations
must support different stakeholders and should not create blind trust in
automated systems~\cite{zocholl_xai_law_enforcement_2025}.

The paper deliberately avoids the predictive-policing direction. Public
National Crime Records Bureau data is useful for background context, but it is
aggregate reported/registered crime data and is not a public individual-level
CCTNS/FIR access-control dataset~\cite{ncrb_2023}. Prior work has also shown
that predictive policing can create feedback loops when observed crime data is
shaped by earlier policing decisions~\cite{ensign_feedback_2018}, and that
complex criminal-justice prediction systems do not automatically outperform
simple baselines~\cite{dressel_fairness_limits_2018}. For these reasons, this
paper studies access governance, auditability, and explanation, not crime
prediction.

The proposed system is named SEBA-XAI: a Secure, Explainable,
Blockchain-Audited overlay for sensitive inter-agency access governance. The
evaluation uses a synthetic workload because real police access logs and
sensitive records are not publicly available. The claim is therefore
controlled and limited. The paper does not claim live deployment inside CCTNS
or ICJS, legal compliance, real police-record validation, production security,
formal privacy, or state-of-the-art performance. Its contribution is an
implemented benchmark and prototype that studies how access control,
blockchain-style audit, and XAI behave together under explicit trust and
visibility assumptions, including baselines, ablations, and failure cases.
