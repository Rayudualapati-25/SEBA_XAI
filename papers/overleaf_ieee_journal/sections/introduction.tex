\section{Introduction}

\IEEEPARstart{C}{onnecting} police databases is largely an engineering exercise.
Governing what happens once they are connected is not. When a sensitive
criminal-justice record becomes technically reachable by a partner agency, the
decisive questions are no longer about transport or storage. They concern
authority and evidence: on what basis was a particular request allowed, denied,
or escalated; which attributes of the requester, the record, and the situation
actually determined that outcome; and what trustworthy trace of the decision
remains for a reviewer who arrives months later, possibly in an adversarial
setting such as a departmental inquiry or a court proceeding. This paper
therefore treats inter-agency police data sharing as an access-governance
problem rather than as an infrastructure problem.

The distinction matters because the infrastructure already exists. The Crime
and Criminal Tracking Network and Systems (CCTNS) is reported by official
sources to be in use across all 17,798 police stations as of 1 February
2026~\cite{pib_cctns_2026}, and the Inter-Operable Criminal Justice System
(ICJS) is designed to carry data exchange between the CCTNS police pillar and
the other criminal-justice pillars, including courts, prisons, forensic
services, and prosecution workflows~\cite{mha_icjs_2026}. The research question
is consequently not whether India needs a replacement for CCTNS or ICJS. It is
narrower and more durable: when a sensitive record moves through an
inter-agency workflow, how can the access decision itself be controlled,
audited, and explained in a form that survives later review?

India supplies the deployment context, but the underlying problem is not
specific to India. Recent international work has independently studied
accountable blockchain-based access control~\cite{hu_accountable_private_ac_2024},
privacy-preserving ABAC~\cite{kerl_privacy_abac_2025}, blockchain protection of
digital evidence~\cite{banerjee_dashdikpala_2026}, data protection for AI in
criminal justice~\cite{anbarasi_ai_criminal_justice_2026}, and explainable AI
for law enforcement~\cite{zocholl_xai_law_enforcement_2025}. Taken together,
these strands establish that access accountability, privacy, evidentiary
integrity, and explanation are all active research problems. Taken
individually, they also expose the gap this paper targets: each strand is
usually advanced in isolation, so a request that is authorised by one
mechanism, logged by a second, and explained by a third is rarely evaluated as
a single governed workflow.

Police records resist generic access control because one request may touch FIR
details, victim or witness identities, juvenile records, forensic material,
cybercrime complaints, case-diary content, evidence references, or
prosecution-linked documents. A coarse role such as ``police officer'' cannot
settle such a request on its own. The same officer may legitimately be
permitted one record, refused another, and referred upward for a third,
depending on jurisdiction, case assignment, stated purpose, sensitivity class,
credential status, approval state, emergency context, and the relationship
between the requesting and holding agencies.

The workflow is also multi-stakeholder, and the stakeholders do not share a
single view of the same request. An investigating police officer requests FIR
details, case-diary material, or evidence references for an assigned case. A
ranked or supervisory officer approves, rejects, or escalates requests that
cross station, district, or sensitivity boundaries. A forensic expert or
laboratory specialist requires controlled access to sample references,
laboratory reports, or evidence-chain records. A prosecutor, court-linked
authority, or auditor arrives after the fact and must verify whether a decision
followed the applicable policy and approval path. This last role is the most
demanding one for system design, because it is separated in time from the
decision it examines: whatever evidence was not recorded at decision time
cannot be recovered at review time. Collectively, these roles motivate an
access model that establishes not only who the requester is, but also what
record was sought, why, under whose authority, and what evidence should persist
for later scrutiny.

The cost of getting this wrong extends to police personnel themselves. In 2023,
a Police Service of Northern Ireland data breach exposed officer and staff
details such as names, ranks, and locations, and the UK Information
Commissioner's Office subsequently imposed a fine over the
incident~\cite{guardian_psni_breach_2023,ico_psni_fine_2024}. The incident
occurred outside India and outside the CCTNS/ICJS setting, which is precisely
what makes it instructive: police information and the metadata surrounding
access to it carry safety, privacy, and accountability risk in any
jurisdiction once disclosure is neither controlled nor reviewable.

A further observation shapes the evaluation reported here. Audit mechanisms
built on signatures, hash chains, or ledgers answer one question well: has the
recorded log been altered since it was written? They do not answer a second and
equally consequential question: does the recorded decision still correspond to
what policy required? An adversary who controls a signing component can
overwrite a decision and then re-sign the resulting event, yielding a log that
is cryptographically impeccable and semantically false. Integrity verification
cannot detect this by construction. Any evaluation that reports tamper
detection alone will therefore return a clean result on exactly the failure
mode that matters most for accountability.

The problem addressed in this paper follows from these observations. Given a
sensitive inter-agency request for a police or criminal-justice record, the
system must decide whether the request is allowed, denied, or escalated, and
must at the same time preserve reviewable evidence of that decision: the
request context, the policy version, the attributes that proved decisive, the
explanation given, and the approval or review event connected to it. Two
constraints bound the design. Raw sensitive records must never be placed on a
shared ledger, and no single mechanism, whether blockchain, access control, or
AI, may be assumed sufficient on its own.

\subsection{Practical Challenges}

The paper is organised around four practical challenges that arise when
sensitive police or criminal-justice records are requested across agencies.
Each challenge is paired with one contribution in the same order.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Contextual access governance.} Inter-agency police access
    cannot be resolved by static roles alone, because the correct decision
    depends jointly on the requester, the record, the action, the stated
    purpose, the approval state, and the operating context.

    \item \textbf{Tamper-evident audit across agencies.} A shared audit trail is
    useful only if later reviewers can trust that decision evidence has not been
    silently altered. That trust must be established without copying raw
    sensitive records onto a common blockchain.

    \item \textbf{Explainable access decisions.} When an officer, an
    investigating agency, a court-related authority, or an auditor seeks a
    sensitive record, the system should be able to state why the request was
    allowed, denied, or escalated. Such decisions bear on investigation
    timelines, privacy protection, inter-agency accountability, and subsequent
    legal or administrative review.

    \item \textbf{Valid-looking corrupted logs.} A stronger adversary can alter
    policy output and re-sign the affected log entry, so that the log remains
    internally valid while the decision it records has been corrupted.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Research Contributions}

To address these challenges, the paper makes four contributions, ordered to
match the challenges above.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Contribution 1:} A layered access-control service for
    CCTNS/ICJS-compatible access requests, combining RBAC, ABAC, and PBAC-style
    rules and returning one of three decisions: \emph{allow}, \emph{deny}, or
    \emph{escalate}.

    \item \textbf{Contribution 2:} A permissioned blockchain-style audit layer
    that records request identifiers, policy versions, decision hashes,
    explanation hashes, approval references, record commitments, and block
    commitments, while keeping raw records off-chain.

    \item \textbf{Contribution 3:} An explainable decision service that records
    the decision label, reason code, decisive attributes, policy or rule
    version, counterfactual information where applicable, and an explanation
    hash bound to the corresponding audit event. The explanation is treated as
    an audit artifact rather than as a message displayed on a screen.

    \item \textbf{Contribution 4:} A reproducible adversarial benchmark
    covering ordinary tamper attacks and a validly re-signed compromised-signer
    attack, comparing mutable logs, signed hash chains, blockchain-style audit,
    CT-style logs, ABAC/Fabric-style re-execution, a trusted raw-attribute
    policy oracle, and NS-PI log-only drift detection.
\end{itemize}

The first contribution is principally about security and access control, the
second about blockchain as an audit mechanism, and the third about explainable
AI. The fourth sharpens the evaluation by exercising a failure mode in which a
log stays cryptographically valid while becoming semantically wrong. Together
they hold the work to access governance rather than to broad claims about
police automation.

\subsection{Design Scope of Each Layer}

Each layer is deliberately used within narrow limits. The access-control layer
follows the ABAC principle that decisions may be based on subject, object,
action, and environmental attributes~\cite{nist_abac_2014}. This suits police
data sharing better than role-only access, because an identical role can demand
different outcomes under different purposes, jurisdictions, and sensitivity
levels. The blockchain layer supplies tamper-evident audit commitments and
provenance, and nothing more: it is not offered as a privacy guarantee and it
stores no raw police records. This restraint is consistent with the cautious
treatment of blockchain access-control systems in
NISTIR~8403~\cite{nist_blockchain_access_2022} and with the permissioned model
represented by Hyperledger Fabric~\cite{androulaki_fabric_2018}. The
explainable AI layer explains access decisions only; it explains neither crime
prediction nor suspect prediction. For each request it records why the request
was allowed, denied, or escalated, which attributes were decisive, and which
policy version applied. This reflects the wider argument that high-stakes
systems should favour interpretable and reviewable decisions wherever
possible~\cite{rudin_2019}, together with recent law-enforcement XAI work
warning that explanations must serve distinct stakeholders and must not induce
blind trust in automated systems~\cite{zocholl_xai_law_enforcement_2025}.

The paper also declines the predictive-policing direction as a matter of
design. Public National Crime Records Bureau data is valuable as background
context, but it reports aggregate registered crime and is not a public
individual-level CCTNS or FIR access-control dataset~\cite{ncrb_2023}. Prior
work has further shown that predictive policing can induce feedback loops when
observed crime data is itself shaped by earlier policing
decisions~\cite{ensign_feedback_2018}, and that complex criminal-justice
prediction systems do not automatically outperform simple
baselines~\cite{dressel_fairness_limits_2018}. The paper accordingly studies
access governance, auditability, and explanation, not crime prediction.

\subsection{Scope of Claims}

The proposed system is named SEBA-XAI: a Secure, Explainable,
Blockchain-Audited overlay for sensitive inter-agency access governance. Its
central empirical finding is an asymmetry. In the adversarial benchmark, every
integrity-based defence, including signed hash chains, blockchain-style audit,
CT-style logs, and ABAC/Fabric-style re-execution, detects ordinary tampering
yet records a detection rate of zero on the validly re-signed
compromised-signer attack. Under different and stronger visibility assumptions,
NS-PI log-only drift detection and a trusted raw-attribute policy oracle detect
that same attack. The conclusion drawn is not that one defence dominates, but
that these mechanisms answer different questions and should therefore be
evaluated together rather than substituted for one another.

Because real police access logs and sensitive records are not publicly
available, the evaluation uses a reproducible synthetic workload, and the
resulting claim is correspondingly bounded. The paper does not claim live
deployment inside CCTNS or ICJS, legal compliance, validation on real police
records, production-grade security, formal privacy, or state-of-the-art
performance. What it offers is an implemented prototype and benchmark that show
how access control, blockchain-style audit, and XAI behave in combination under
explicit trust and visibility assumptions, with baselines, ablations, and
failure cases reported alongside the positive results.
