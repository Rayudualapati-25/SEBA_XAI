## Introduction

India's police and criminal-justice information systems are no longer a purely local or paper-based environment. The Crime and Criminal Tracking Network and Systems (CCTNS) provides a national digital backbone for police processes, and a 2026 Press Information Bureau release reports that all 17,798 police stations were using CCTNS as of 2026-02-01. The same official source states that CCTNS supports digitization of police processes including FIRs and chargesheets, state-hosted application deployment, near-real-time replication to the National Data Centre, search of crime, criminal, and property information, standardized Integrated Investigation Forms, and master codes for states, districts, police stations, acts, and sections [PIB-CCTNS-2026]. The Inter-Operable Criminal Justice System (ICJS) further places police data within a broader criminal-justice ecosystem connecting CCTNS with courts, prisons, forensics, and prosecution [MHA-ICJS-2026]. These developments make the research problem one of trustworthy augmentation rather than replacement.

The existence of such infrastructure does not remove the access-governance problem. Police and criminal-justice records can contain FIR details, witness statements, victim information, juvenile records, forensic reports, cybercrime complaints, case diary material, evidence references, and court or prosecution-linked records. A request for such data is not valid merely because the requester belongs to a police agency. Its legitimacy can depend on role, rank, jurisdiction, case assignment, purpose, record sensitivity, time window, credential status, emergency context, and superior or judicial authorization. This creates a need for an auditable access workflow that can later reconstruct who requested what, under which policy, with what approval, and for what stated purpose.

This paper treats blockchain, security/privacy/access control, and explainable AI as equal pillars of that workflow. Blockchain is useful when multiple organizations need a tamper-evident record of requests, approvals, policy versions, and explanation commitments. NIST describes blockchain access-control systems in terms of decentralization, high confidence, and tamper-resistance, while also treating them as systems with implementation trade-offs rather than automatic security guarantees [NIST-BC-AC-2022]. Hyperledger Fabric is a relevant candidate because it is a permissioned blockchain platform designed for known organizations rather than open anonymous participation [FABRIC-2018]. However, blockchain alone does not make police data private, correct, fair, or legally admissible. Sensitive records should remain off-chain, with only hashes, policy identifiers, approval proofs, model versions, explanation hashes, and audit metadata committed to the ledger.

The access-control layer is therefore central. NIST SP 800-162 defines attribute-based access control (ABAC) as authorization based on attributes of the subject, object, requested operation, and environment evaluated against policy rules or relationships [NIST-ABAC-2014]. This model fits inter-agency police access better than static role-based access alone because a request can be legitimate in one context and inappropriate in another. A superior officer requesting a forensic report for an assigned case during an active investigation is different from an officer outside jurisdiction requesting a sensitive victim record without case assignment or approval. The proposed system therefore uses ABAC or policy-based access control to represent role, jurisdiction, case assignment, record sensitivity, purpose, credential status, and approval state as explicit policy inputs.

Explainable AI is needed because access decisions and risk scores in high-stakes public-safety workflows must be reviewable. Recent law-enforcement XAI work emphasizes stakeholder-specific explanation needs, AI literacy, and automation-bias risks [XAI-LE-2025], while high-stakes AI literature cautions against relying on opaque models when interpretable alternatives are feasible [RUDIN-2019]. In this paper, XAI is not treated as a dashboard decoration or as a guarantee of trust. It is treated as an auditable artifact: the system should be able to explain why a request was allowed, denied, or escalated; which policy attributes mattered; what data was missing; whether an AI risk score influenced review; and whether a human reviewer overrode the model or policy recommendation.

Prior work covers important parts of this problem but not the complete combination required here. Blockchain has been studied for digital crime evidence management and lawful evidence workflows [EVIDENCE-BC-2021], [LECHAIN-2021]. Fabric-based ABAC has been studied for generic data-sharing systems [FABRIC-ABAC-2022]. XAI and fairness literature has identified risks in high-stakes and predictive-policing contexts, including feedback loops that can arise when reported police data shapes future enforcement patterns [FEEDBACK-2018]. India-specific public crime analytics commonly uses aggregate NCRB data, but public aggregate statistics cannot support individual suspect prediction or direct operational access-control claims [NCRB-2023]. The gap is a reproducible, CCTNS/ICJS-compatible access-governance overlay that jointly evaluates permissioned blockchain audit, contextual authorization, superior approval, off-chain sensitive storage, XAI artifact logging, tamper detection, metadata leakage, and explanation stability.

To address this gap, this paper proposes SEBA-XAI, a Secure, Explainable, Blockchain-Audited Access Overlay for inter-agency police data sharing in India. SEBA-XAI assumes that existing police and criminal-justice systems remain authoritative for raw records. The overlay receives access requests, evaluates contextual policy attributes, logs audit commitments on a permissioned ledger, stores sensitive records and explanation text off-chain, and records hashes for policies, approvals, model versions, payload pointers, and explanation artifacts. For sensitive records, the system supports allow, deny, or escalate decisions, with final disclosure remaining subject to authorized human approval.

The intended contributions are fourfold. First, the paper formulates a CCTNS/ICJS-compatible access-governance problem for sensitive inter-agency police record sharing. Second, it proposes SEBA-XAI as an overlay combining permissioned blockchain audit commitments, ABAC/PBAC, off-chain encrypted storage, and XAI artifact logging. Third, it defines a reproducible synthetic multi-station workload and evaluation plan for allow, deny, and escalate decisions. Fourth, it identifies evaluation metrics for audit completeness, tamper detection, false allows and denies, metadata leakage, latency, throughput, and explanation stability.

The scope of the paper is deliberately limited. It does not propose replacing CCTNS or ICJS, storing raw police records on-chain, proving legal compliance, claiming deployment readiness, or asserting state-of-the-art crime prediction. Public NCRB and BPRD datasets may support aggregate context and careful trend analysis, but they do not justify individual suspect prediction or unrestricted police-record modeling. The remainder of the paper presents the background and related work, threat model, SEBA-XAI architecture, evaluation methodology, ethics and legal-risk analysis, and limitations.
